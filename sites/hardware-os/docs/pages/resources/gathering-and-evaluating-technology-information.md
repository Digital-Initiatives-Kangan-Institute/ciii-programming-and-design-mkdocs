# Gathering and Evaluating Technology Information

Before an organisation adopts a new technology, it needs information: what the technology does, whether it will fit, and whether it can be trusted. Research involves two related skills — knowing where to look, and knowing how to judge what you find.

---

## Where Information Comes From

Good research rarely relies on a single source. Different kinds of sources answer different questions, so it helps to gather information from several places and compare them.

### The people who make it

Vendors publish technical specifications, installation guides, user manuals, release notes, and compatibility lists. This is the best place to learn a product's exact capabilities, requirements, and supported configurations.

Keep in mind that vendors are also selling something. Their technical documentation is usually reliable, but marketing pages and slogans should be treated separately from specifications.

### The wider industry

Industry publications, technology news sites, standards organisations, and analyst reports give a broader view. They help you see whether a technology is being widely adopted, what alternatives exist, and where the industry is heading.

### The people who use it

Online communities, forums, review platforms, and colleagues who already use the technology provide real-world experience — what works well, what breaks, and what the documentation does not tell you.

### Experts and peers

Experienced ICT professionals and subject matter experts can give direct, contextual advice. A short conversation with someone who has deployed the technology before can save hours of reading, especially when judging whether it suits a particular business environment.

### First-hand evaluation

There is no substitute for trying something yourself. Trial versions, free tiers, demos, or a small pilot deployment answer questions that no document can — how the technology actually behaves in your own environment.

---

## Judging Whether Information Is Worth Trusting

Not everything online is accurate, current, or relevant. Before relying on a source, it helps to ask a few questions.

- **Who produced it, and why?** A reputable vendor, standards body, or technical authority carries more weight than an anonymous blog. Consider whether the source has a reason to be biased.
- **How old is it?** Technology changes quickly. Check when the information was published or last updated, and whether it still reflects the current version of the product.
- **Does it actually apply?** Information about the wrong product, version, or environment is noise. Confirm that what you are reading matches the hardware, software, or operating system you are investigating.
- **Is it supported by evidence?** Look for claims backed by technical documentation, recognised standards, or test results, rather than opinion or marketing.
- **Do other sources agree?** Cross-check against independent, credible sources. When several reliable sources point the same way, you can be more confident; when they conflict, dig deeper.

The goal is not to find one perfect source. It is to build confidence by combining information that is credible, current, relevant, and corroborated. If a source fails one of these tests, it should be treated with caution.

---

## Summary

- Gather information from vendors, industry sources, user communities, experts, and hands-on testing
- Each source has strengths and limitations, so compare several rather than relying on one
- Judge information by asking who produced it, how current it is, whether it applies, and whether it is supported
- Cross-check claims across independent sources before acting on them
